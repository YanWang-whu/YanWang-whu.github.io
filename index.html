My Visit

Shandong Yinfeng Life Science Research Institute

During my visit, the institute's director described rabbit-heart preservation and rewarming experiments, including cases where beating activity resumed. I am recording this as an account from our conversation, rather than as a published result.

I also learned about the institute's human cryopreservation program, which was described to me in terms of body donation and research.

The visit made me want to understand what counts as recovery in an organ. Beating again, maintaining a coordinated rhythm, and sustaining pumping function are different endpoints. I would want to know which was measured and for how long.

It also helped me separate two interests: preserving an organ and changing the physiological state of a living animal. Torpor interests me because it involves coordinated changes across the body. How an animal enters that state and returns to normal is the question I want to keep following.

The question

I am studying how PM20D1 and the long noncoding RNA PM20D1-AS1 are expressed in Parkinson’s disease. Much of my work focuses on a practical question: when a gene has several transcripts, which ones can we actually distinguish in the available data and in our experiments?

What I do

For PM20D1-AS1, I mapped transcript annotations, compared expression across normal tissues, and examined public Parkinson’s brain datasets by donor and cell type. I also looked at patterns across disease stages. I keep transcripts that the data cannot resolve separate from those with no detected expression.

At the bench, I work on primer mapping and qPCR checks, including dilution series and reverse-transcription controls. For PM20D1, I am checking which public datasets have the read coverage and splice-junction evidence needed for transcript-level questions.

What I am working out

The analyses are still in progress. A pattern across donors is a starting point for follow-up, and does not by itself tell us what an RNA does. This project has made me more careful about matching the question to what an assay can measure, especially when computational and qPCR results appear to disagree.

I study how INS-1 changes ethanol responses in C. elegans, using behavioral genetics and calcium imaging.

SfN 2026 · Abstract submitted

View the abstract citation

The Question

The same smell or taste can lead to different behavior depending on an animal's state. I use C. elegans to study how a neuropeptide helps produce one such change: the response to ethanol at different salt concentrations.

In our assays, worms are attracted to ethanol under low-salt conditions and avoid it under high-salt conditions. ins-1 mutants are attracted to ethanol even under high salt. I wanted to find out which neurons contribute INS-1, how it affects the circuit, and which receptor might be involved.

What I Did

This is the first project I have taken through most of the experimental process myself, with guidance from my mentors. I started with chemotaxis assays and then used cell-specific genetics and calcium imaging to work back from the behavior.

  1. Test candidate source neurons. I generated cell-specific ins-1 knockouts using loxP/Cre and complementary rescue lines in ASEL, ASER, AWC, AIY, AIZ, and other candidate cells, then quantified high-salt ethanol chemotaxis across genotypes.
  2. Measure and perturb circuit activity. I established ethanol-gradient calcium imaging in ASEL, ASER, and AIY, and combined optogenetic manipulation with PH-miniSOG-mediated neuronal ablation to test the functional contribution of upstream and downstream nodes.
  3. Test possible signaling pathways. I phenotyped canonical insulin-pathway mutants (daf-2, age-1, akt-1/2, daf-16, and daf-18), tested the candidate neurotensin-like GPCR ntr-1, and integrated behavioral genetics with structure prediction and protein docking.
What We Have Found So Far
01 · Several neurons contribute

The cell-specific experiments point to several contributing neurons. ASEL is supported by deletion and rescue experiments; AIY by deletion, rescue, and ablation; AIZ by rescue; and AIB by ablation. These tests ask different questions: rescue can show that expression in a neuron is sufficient, while ablation tests the contribution of the neuron as a whole. They do not all establish where INS-1 is normally released.

02 · ASER responds at a higher ethanol concentration

The ASER response peak shifts from about 0.5% ethanol in wild-type N2 to about 1.5% in ins-1 mutants. The negative calcium response in AIY is also reduced. Higher ethanol concentrations partly restore avoidance, which is consistent with a change in the response range rather than a complete loss of ethanol sensing.

03 · ASEL rescue partly restores the ASER response

Expressing ins-1 in ASEL shifts the ASER response peak from about 1.5% to about 1.0% ethanol. This partial rescue suggests that INS-1 from ASEL can influence the ethanol response in ASER.

04 · NTR-1 is a receptor candidate

The canonical insulin-pathway mutants we tested did not show the same behavior as ins-1 mutants, while ntr-1 mutants showed a similar defect. I am testing NTR-1 as a possible receptor. Structure predictions help us choose follow-up experiments, but direct INS-1 binding and receptor activation still need to be tested.

Current idea: INS-1 from several neurons may help set the ethanol-response range of ASER and influence downstream AIY activity. NTR-1 could be involved in this process. We are still testing how these observations fit together.

What I Learned

I worked on plasmid design, microinjection, strain construction, chemotaxis assays, calcium imaging, optogenetics, neuronal ablation, and structural modeling. The behavior was sensitive to small changes in the assay. I paid particular attention to ethanol evaporation, humidity, temperature, and timing, and used intermittent illumination to limit heating during optogenetic experiments.

The most useful part of this project has been learning when two experiments support the same explanation and when they do not. A rescue result, a calcium trace, and a similar mutant phenotype each tell me something different. I am learning to keep those differences in mind when deciding what to test next.

I use yeast display and directed evolution to improve a protein-labeling tool, then compare promising PafA variants in follow-up assays.

2025 Candidate Project

National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC) Young Student Basic Research Program (Undergraduate Track) Applicant: Wang, Y. · Project contributors: Zhang, J., Wang, Y.

Background

A small molecule can change a cell's behavior before we know which proteins it binds. Our lab developed POST-IT to help identify these proteins by labeling them near a small-molecule probe.

POST-IT uses HaloTag to connect a small-molecule probe to the labeling system. The enzyme PafA attaches SBP–Pup to nearby proteins so that they can be recovered for analysis. Our project focuses on a practical limitation: labeling can be slow and requires a relatively high substrate concentration.

Research

We used error-prone PCR and yeast surface display to screen PafA variants for stronger labeling within a shorter time and at a lower substrate concentration.

We expressed and purified PafA and SBP–Pup, built a mutant library of approximately 106 variants, and carried out nine rounds of flow-cytometry sorting while shortening the labeling time. The R9 library gave a stronger labeling signal under the screening conditions.

Sequencing the enriched library identified nine candidate mutation sites. We combined them into five PafA variants and compared labeling by flow cytometry and confocal imaging. The 9mt-L1 variant, which carries all nine mutations, performed best in these assays. Further screening allowed us to shorten the tested reaction conditions from 18 h at 40 μM substrate to 2 h at 10 μM.

I also used AlphaFold3 and structural inspection to look at where the mutations fall relative to the dimer interface and the ATP-binding and catalytic regions. These models suggest possible explanations to test; they do not show how the mutations work.

The next step is to test the improved system with dasatinib and then with Hino, a compound being studied in our lab in relation to Parkinson's disease. We plan to prepare a Hino-linked probe and use POST-IT to look for candidate target proteins.

My Participation

I began this project with a senior student and another undergraduate. The two of us later took on the day-to-day experiments, sharing much of the work. My experiments included mutant-plasmid construction, yeast preparation, protein purification, in vitro labeling assays, SDS–PAGE, cellular labeling, and flow cytometry.

One problem I remember well was getting enough SBP–Pup. PafA purification worked, but after five attempts we still did not have a usable amount of SBP–Pup. We tried different columns and IPTG concentrations and used Western blots to check expression. Switching to ArcticExpress cells and low-temperature expression finally gave us usable protein. We suspected a folding or solubility problem, although we did not establish the exact cause.

2025 iGEM Gold Medal

Awarded a Gold Medal at the 2025 International Genetically Engineered Machine (iGEM) Competition.

Background

Putting many logic gates in one cell can become difficult because the circuits share cellular resources and can interfere with one another. I was interested in a different approach: could separate bacterial colonies carry out parts of a calculation and communicate through diffusible signals?

Work by Fedorec and colleagues showed how colonies with high-pass or band-pass responses could form logic gates on solid media. The arrangement of the colonies and the diffusion of the signals became part of the circuit. This was the starting point for our project.

We combined several quorum-sensing systems with spatially arranged colonies to build logic gates and a binary adder. We also explored light-controlled signal and protein degradation as a way to reset the system.

Research

We constructed fluorescent reporters from six different quorum-sensing systems and tested their orthogonality, obtaining two sets of three orthogonal systems each to support inputs, outputs, and carry signals for the adder.

For each quorum-sensing system, we measured the diffusion–response curves on solid media and built high-pass and band-pass circuits, allowing engineered cells to perform basic logic gates like AND, OR, and XOR. We further designed and implemented cascaded logic gate experiments to simulate multi-input logic, serial calculation and addition computation.

To enable repeated operation, we inserted light-sensitive domains into the AHL-degrading enzyme AiiA for light-controlled degradation and developed light-inducible fluorescent proteins. This involved functional validation of the enzyme, molecular dynamics simulations of AiiA, and functional validation of the protein degradation tag.

For accurate and automated quantification of fluorescent colonies, we developed a colony quantification program. We also modeled wet-lab data for different quorum-sensing systems, creating simulation software to predict system responses to AHLs, and an educational platform to simulate colonies forming an adder.

We brought the biosensors, diffusion measurements, logic-gate designs, light-controlled degradation tools, models, software, and colony-positioning hardware together in the LOGIC toolbox. The designs and results are available on our team wiki.

My Participation

I proposed the LOGIC concept: using multiple quorum-sensing systems and spatial diffusion to connect logic inputs and build an adder. I also drew the first modular plan for the project.

My wet-lab work included screening quorum-sensing systems for cross-talk, testing individual and cascaded logic gates, and running multi-input experiments. I also quantified and plotted the logic-gate results and helped put the toolbox together.

Two of my proposals, LOGIC and CLEANSE, reached the final four from more than 20 team proposals. LOGIC grew out of a microbiology lesson on quorum sensing. I wondered whether bacterial signals could act as the “wires” between colonies, and whether arranging the colonies could help us do a calculation.

We needed to compare fluorescence across many colonies, signal concentrations, distances, and time points. I learned ImageJ macros and wrote batch-processing scripts to identify and measure colonies. We used them to process more than 6,000 colonies and 20,000 measurements.
